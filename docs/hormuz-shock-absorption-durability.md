# How durable are the buffers absorbing the Hormuz oil shock?

**Evidence cutoff:** historical global accounting through 30 June 2026; selected U.S.
stock observations through 7 August; national-stock publications and public OMR evidence
through 17 August 2026. All figures cover oil
and other petroleum liquids, not LNG. The forward discussion is conditional on Strait oil
traffic remaining around the current partial-flow regime. It is not a price forecast.

## Bottom line

The historical March-June market-clearing bridge is still about **1,441.5 million barrels**:
439.2 mb of consumption below the frozen February forecast, 396.1 mb of an expected stock
build that never occurred, 298.0 mb of observed inventory draw, and a 308.2 mb
implied-versus-observed stock residual. Those four quantities close arithmetically, but they
do not have equal evidentiary strength or equal durability.

Three conclusions change the forward interpretation materially:

1. **The 396.1 mb foregone build is fully spent.** It was a one-time benefit of entering the
   shock with a projected surplus. It is not oil in a tank and cannot contribute again.
2. **Bypass is durable in time but almost capped in rate.** The reconstructed June increment
   is about 3.4 mb/d, with only a 0.2 mb/d low-confidence Iraq sensitivity. Saudi Arabia's
   reported 7 mb/d is Petroline throughput, while the IEA's greater-than-5 mb/d number is
   Yanbu exports; subtracting one from the other would manufacture nonexistent headroom.
   Since 20 July, the Houthis' declared Saudi-specific Bab el-Mandeb embargo and subsequent
   attacks have degraded the southbound delivery leg, but they have not sealed the strait or
   stopped Yanbu loading. Kpler still observed Saudi crude transits after the declaration;
   Saudi Arabia also shifted cargoes north through Suez/SUMED. Treat this as a route-delay,
   insurance and destination-access constraint, not as a verified 3.4 mb/d loss of bypass.
3. **Inventory is genuinely exhaustible, so publish the bounds that are computable.** The
   U.S. SPR's fixed-rate upper bound is about 465 days to physical zero at its latest draw
   rate; its operational floor binds no later. For the IEA aggregate, 1,000 mb divided by
   reported collective rates gives 400-455 days, but the stock figure is a lower bound and
   the flow mixes public and obligated-industry barrels, so these are illustrative zero
   calculations rather than strict aggregate upper bounds.

This means the cheap absorbers do not all “run out” on one date. Instead, the marginal barrel
shifts gradually toward demand adjustment, while non-Gulf supply improves slowly and bypass
continues unless a concentrated route fails.

## Two ledgers, not one pile of offsets

Rerouting and replacement production sit **upstream** of the net supply loss: they prevent
some missing Hormuz barrels from becoming a global shortage. Stocks, foregone builds and
demand changes sit **downstream**: they explain how the remaining shortage cleared. Adding
the two sides would double count.

| Channel | Historical March-June contribution | Current durability interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Incremental Gulf bypass | 362.1 mb upstream; about 3.4 mb/d in June | Historical total is unchanged because the Bab el-Mandeb escalation began after June. Forward flow is near its rate ceiling and now faces realized Red Sea interdiction risk; northbound Suez/SUMED rerouting preserves some volume at materially longer transit times. |
| Net non-Gulf production revision | 56.9 mb upstream; 0.47 mb/d historical average | Slow, durable improvement; the existing central scenario reaches about 1 mb/d by March 2027, but that is not an engineering guarantee |
| Consumption below February path | 439.2 mb downstream before China sensitivity | Scalable but economically heterogeneous; installed switching can persist, while industrial curtailment and essential-fuel scarcity become progressively more damaging |
| Foregone expected build | 396.1 mb downstream | **Zero remaining durable credit** |
| Observed global inventory draw | 298.0 mb downstream | Exhaustible; global usable headroom and sustainable release rate remain unknown |
| Unreconciled implied-versus-observed adjustment | 308.2 mb base; 2.8-413.7 mb range | Unknown mechanism and therefore unknown remaining capacity; it cannot be projected |

The broader confidence ledger classifies **422.3 mb, or 29.3%**, of historical absorption as
T4 “unknown” in its base case. That total includes both the 308.2 mb reconciliation plug and
114.1 mb of ordinary-demand-revision/counterfactual uncertainty nested in the demand slice.
Its durability must remain unknown rather than being assumed exhausted or infinite.

The follow-up “missing barrels” audit rejects the tempting claim that a 300 mb discrepancy
over four months is normal. The famous 1998 H1 gap was a comparable **325.7 mb only over six
months**, at 1.799 mb/d and with the opposite sign; the current 2.526 mb/d rate is 40% faster.
Same-sign 2003 episodes were about 1.6-1.7 mb/d. The defensible description is therefore **a
large, historically legible preliminary-balance discrepancy, but not a normal one and not
308.2 mb of measured hidden barrels**. Historical revisions, agency scatter and GAO's audit
all point to a mixture of model/vintage error and unobserved non-OECD, producer, underground
or in-transit stocks; none identifies the split. The mechanism ranking puts model/vintage
error and understated demand or refinery cuts first, with a massive Chinese government-SPR
draw remaining a low-support hypothesis.

## Inventory: what is actually known

The project can separate gross levels, release rates and usable headroom, but it cannot fill
all three cells globally.

- The latest public quantified IEA statement remains the 21 July report that member
  countries had released **around 290 million barrels** and still held **more than 1 billion
  barrels** of government-controlled stocks. The 12 August OMR says the pace of emergency
  releases slowed in July but publishes neither a newer cumulative delivery total nor a
  newer government-stock level. At the May collective-action pace of 2.5 mb/d,
  1,000 mb is 400 days; at the 11 March-21 July average of 2.197 mb/d, it is 455 days.
  Because the level is *more than* 1,000 mb and the release includes obligated-industry
  barrels, these are lower bounds on hypothetical zero arithmetic, not strict upper bounds
  on the unknown aggregate zero date.
- The latest free IEA net-import-cover table, for April, reports net-importing members at
  **136 days total: 78 industry plus 58 public**. The 90-day rule therefore requires at
  least 12 aggregate public days if industry cover is fully usable, leaving at most 46
  public days above the aggregate obligation. Country law and non-fungibility make the
  usable public-stock bound **0-46 days**, and the April result is not a July tank balance.
- The U.S. SPR stood at **298.694 mb on 7 August**, down 26.961 mb from 26 June, an average
  six-week draw of **0.6419 mb/d**. At that rate, every last barrel would be gone in
  **465.3 days, or 15.3 months, around 15 November 2027**. At the March-June calendar rate
  of 0.736 mb/d, the corresponding upper bound is 405.9 days, around 16 September 2027.
  Put plainly: **the U.S. SPR cannot sustain the latest draw beyond roughly 15 months even
  if every last barrel were usable; real operational and policy limits can only shorten
  that window.**
- Full EIA weekly history shows that 298.694 mb is the **lowest U.S. SPR level since
  28 January 1983**, more than 43 years. The IEA April table classifies the United States
  as a net exporter, so the IEA 90-day rule creates no positive statutory U.S. SPR floor;
  the relevant U.S. floor is operational and political.
- GAO's latest audited DOE snapshot shows why physical zero is permissive. In December
  2025, effective draw capability was **2.700 mb/d versus 4.415 mb/d design**, and more
  than one-quarter of the 413 mb inventory snapshot was unavailable because of construction
  and cavern outages. DOE separately says 4.4 mb/d lasts at most 90 days before declining.
  No current public numeric curve maps total inventory to effective withdrawal rate.
- U.S. commercial petroleum excluding SPR stood at **1,236.468 mb on 7 August** and had
  **built 34.898 mb** since 26 June, about 0.831 mb/d. This stronger reversal is useful evidence that
  commercial stock draw is not a fixed burn rate. It says little by itself about China or
  the global residual.
- Japan's 17 August report upgrades the national evidence from a days-derived estimate to
  exact June month-end product-equivalent volumes. From February through June, national
  stocks drew **71.955 mb**, obligated private stocks built **6.793 mb**, and producer-country
  joint stocks drew **6.479 mb**, for a signed net draw of **71.641 mb**. This net movement
  is nested inside observed global stocks and must not be added to the IEA's gross release
  total.
- Historical cases support only process-scale transfer: coordinated releases have been
  scheduled over roughly **30-180 days**, while emergency-stock obligations have taken about
  **12-18 months** to normalize. U.S. direct-purchase refill after 2022 ran only about
  **0.06-0.10 mb/d**. None is a current global headroom estimate.

### Oil on water is accounting inventory, but not accessible inventory

The follow-up stock bridge corrects an important premise. Global oil on water **fell 117 mb
in March**; the previously cited +100 mb was a Middle East Gulf floating-storage build nested
inside that global decline, not a global build. The matched monthly bridge is:

| Month | Total stock change | Oil-on-water change | Onshore change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| March | -129 mb | -117 mb | -12 mb |
| April | -117 | +53 | -170 |
| May | -73 | -35 / 0 / +35 sensitivity | -38 / -73 / -108 |
| June | +21 | +117 | -96 |

That implies an onshore-accessible March-June draw of **316 / 351 / 386 mb**, versus the
298 mb total-stock headline. The wider mixed-vintage envelope is 273-456 mb. This makes the
durability position worse—accessible tanks supplied more oil while barrels were tied up in
the maritime pipeline—but it does **not** close the 308.171 mb balance plug. EIA's implied
draw and IEA's observed comparator both include oil on water; removing it from only one side
changes the boundary. The valid same-bound closure from this reclassification is zero.

The causal decomposition also separates two route effects that should not be conflated:

- Persistent longer-route inflation required an estimated **57-125 mb**, base 91 mb, of
  additional voyage float by end-June: Atlantic crude moving East of Suez plus longer Yanbu
  routings. Russia's 2022 rerouting and the 2024 Red Sea diversions independently validate a
  tens-of-millions-of-barrels order of magnitude.
- June's partial reopening separately refilled the ordinary Hormuz transit system. The
  estimated **66 / 111.6 / 151.8 mb** refill explains 56-100% of the +117 mb June build,
  base 95%, leaving +51 / +5.4 / -34.8 mb as discretionary or other change. Persistent
  route inflation and system refill are not automatically additive across time.

March was different again: approximately -181 mb of oil in transit, +100 mb of blocked Gulf
floating storage and -36 mb of other/vintage effects. Strong March-June backwardation—WTI
front-minus-December positive on 84/84 observations and Brent on 82/84—makes profitable
contango carry an implausible main explanation. It does not exclude involuntarily blocked
cargoes, congestion, sanctioned-fleet dwell or voyage float. Public vessel-level geography
is insufficient to force the remaining barrels into one cause. For durability, all voyage
float receives **zero usable-headroom credit**.

## China: demand reduction or hidden product destocking?

China's often-cited **119.99 mb** demand gap is March-July and includes **29.05 mb of July
forecast**. The historical March-June denominator is **90.94 mb**.

The hypothesis that part of this is hidden destocking is plausible, but it is not observed.
EIA describes Chinese demand as apparent consumption, and JODI does not receive Chinese
product-stock levels or changes. The reported roughly 25 mb commercial draw concerned crude;
crude entering a refinery is not proof that final product consumption was omitted. Meanwhile,
March-June refinery-run shortfall was about 155.0 mb and export controls retained about
35.2 mb of broad refined products domestically, demonstrating why crude runs cannot be read
as final use.

The recommended low/base/high sensitivity therefore moves **0 / 14.97 / 29.94 mb** from
China demand to an opaque product-stock/reconciliation bucket. The midpoint is judgmental,
not a best estimate. It is not added to the 298 mb observed inventory draw, and the total
1,441.5 mb absorption bridge is unchanged.

## Demand durability is an economic-cost ordering

The country matrix does not invent barrel shares. It classifies mechanisms by what it costs
to sustain them:

- **Tier 1:** installed switching or efficiency that preserves activity, such as existing
  EVs, electric rail, public transit and already-available fuel substitution. It can persist,
  but crisis-induced incremental headroom is usually unmeasured and capped by installed
  capacity.
- **Tier 2:** continuous economic drag without a single failure date—lower refinery and
  petrochemical utilization, reduced aviation, shorter business hours, constrained mobility
  and similar output or convenience losses.
- **Tier 3:** major disruption or essential-service failure—cooking-fuel scarcity, hard
  rationing, lost schooling or livelihoods, plant shutdowns, and freight/food-system failure.

Current high-value examples include tier-2 refinery and petrochemical cuts in China, Japan
and South Korea; tier-3 cooking-LPG exposure in India and Kenya; rationing in Myanmar; school
and work-schedule changes in Lao PDR; small-fishing livelihood losses in the Philippines;
and an emerging tier-2-to-tier-3 freight and food risk in fragile African importers.

The matched country denominator is now explicit. Of the **439.23 mb** worldwide March-June
gap, exact STEO country rows give China 90.94 mb, India 33.54 mb and Japan 16.23 mb. Public
STEO does not expose the country detail needed to split the **134.86 mb Middle East** or
**115.38 mb residual Asia/Oceania** buckets. A deliberately low-fidelity allocation by 2024
petroleum-consumption share places the largest Middle East portions in Saudi Arabia
(52.39 mb), Iran (28.43), Iraq (15.31) and the UAE (12.67). South Korea's 24.40 mb remains
an existing project imputation nested inside residual Asia; after removing it, the same
neutral scaffold places the largest named portions in Indonesia (14.53 mb), Singapore
(13.23), Thailand (12.25), Australia (10.22) and Taiwan (7.78). These numbers identify where
pressure might land for scenario work; they are not observed country losses. JODI March-May
year-on-year apparent demand is retained only as a differently defined, non-additive
cross-check.

## The published forward surplus is mostly a reopening branch

The July STEO does show recovery: its global balance first turns positive in October 2026 at
+2.362 mb/d and averages **+5.031 mb/d in 2027**. But the report explicitly assumes that the
18 June memorandum opens the Strait, with most crude production and trade near pre-conflict
levels by end-2026 and most remaining shut-ins returning in 2027 Q1. It carries Gulf crude
shut-ins down from 5.427 mb/d in Q3 to 1.440 mb/d in Q4. Treating the +5.031 mb/d as an
independent cushion would therefore assume the reopening that the continued-closure scenario
is meant to test.

A bounded counterfactual that holds Q3-level shut-ins while retaining the July demand path
leaves only **-0.396 to +0.502 mb/d** in 2027, midpoint +0.053 mb/d. Holding June shut-ins
instead leaves -3.259 to -2.361 mb/d. The primary hold is already generous because Q3 embeds
post-June improvement. The correct bookkeeping is: **zero already-held buffer credit from
the forward surplus; +5.031 mb/d in EIA's reopening branch; approximately balanced in the
continued-Q3-disruption branch, with at most +0.502 mb/d of optimistic conditional flow.**
That flow is not oil in storage and is not assured headroom.

Source: EIA [July STEO report](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/archives/jul26.pdf)
and [workbook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/archives/jul26_base.xlsx).

## If demand adjustment has to scale

The historical March-June demand-vintage gap is **439.228 mb over 122 days, or 3.600
mb/d**. Three illustrative scales make the forward stakes concrete:

| Demand-adjustment scale | Total | Gross increment | Residual increment after identified new non-demand flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.25× | 4.500 mb/d | +0.900 mb/d | 0.198-0.900 mb/d; base 0.847 |
| 1.50× | 5.400 mb/d | +1.800 mb/d | 1.098-1.800 mb/d; base 1.747 |
| 2.00× | 7.200 mb/d | +3.600 mb/d | 2.898-3.600 mb/d; base 3.547 |

The non-demand envelope is only **0 / 0.053 / 0.702 mb/d** low/base/high: the positive
part of the bounded no-reopening balance plus, only at the high edge, Iraq's undemonstrated
0.2 mb/d Ceyhan sensitivity. The roughly 3.4 mb/d of bypass already running and the 2.197
mb/d historical-average collective stock execution are existing absorbers, not fresh
headroom, so they are not counted again. Additional global stock draw-rate capacity is
unknown and receives no credit.

There is consequently no honest single date on which “non-demand headroom” expires. At the
2.197 mb/d historical collective rate, the IEA's *greater-than* 1 bn barrel gross level puts
physical zero no earlier than 19 October 2027, while legal, product, ownership and operating
floors can bind much sooner. The U.S. fixed-rate physical-zero endpoint of 24 December 2027
is likewise a latest gross bound, not an operating forecast. As a mechanical sensitivity
only, losing a 2.197 mb/d stock flow would raise total required demand adjustment to 6.697,
7.597 and 9.397 mb/d in the three rows above.

Where the increment lands is less precise than the arithmetic. A neutral continuity
benchmark based on the positive historical country gaps assigns 28.9% to Northeast Asian
refining and petrochemicals, 29.6% to fragile and diversified Gulf economies together,
12.3% to Southeast Asia, 7.4% to India, 7.7% to other Asia/Oceania, 5.8% to Africa and 8.2%
to other positive regions. This is a closing allocation scaffold, not a forecast. The
country-sector evidence supplies the more defensible ordering:

- At **1.25×**, the extra 0.9 mb/d is still tier-2 sized: it most plausibly deepens refinery,
  petrochemical, aviation and discretionary-mobility losses, while widening existing LPG
  and livelihood pockets.
- At **1.5×**, efficiency and installed switching are not a credible full explanation.
  Northeast Asian and Gulf industrial/service cuts deepen; cooking-fuel, freight and food
  stress spreads across import-dependent economies.
- At **2×**, the increment equals the entire historical demand adjustment again. Broad
  transport and industrial contraction plus materially more tier-3 essential-service
  failure becomes more plausible than additional voluntary conservation alone.

The tier-3 watchlist is therefore concrete even though its future barrel shares are not:
Indian and Kenyan cooking LPG; Myanmar rationing; Lao schooling and work schedules;
Philippine fishing livelihoods; fragile Gulf activity; and freight and food systems in
low-fiscal-space African importers. Northeast Asian industry is likely to carry larger
absolute volumes, but the same number of barrels can cause much greater welfare loss in
these smaller, more constrained systems.

## Conditional marginal-absorber timeline

| Horizon from 5 Aug 2026 | Conditional composition under continued current Strait traffic |
|---|---|
| 6 months (5 Feb 2027) | Bypass remains near its ceiling, non-Gulf supply ramps slowly, and remaining inventory is policy-gated. The demand margin is mainly tier-2 refining, petrochemical, aviation and mobility drag, with tier-3 pockets already present in cooking fuel, rationing, schooling and livelihoods. |
| 12 months (5 Aug 2027) | Low-cost switching supplies persistence, not much new headroom. Inventory cannot be assumed replenished. Tier-2 industrial and service losses accumulate, and fiscally constrained/import-dependent economies increasingly cross into tier 3. Any forecast surplus that assumes reopening or recovery is not credited here. |
| 18 months (5 Feb 2028) | Current stock-draw rates and unidentified T4 absorption are no longer extrapolable. Richer systems can invest into more tier-1 substitution and sustain tier-2 drag; in low-fiscal-space and infrastructure-constrained economies, the marginal demand absorber becomes predominantly tier 3. This date is beyond the public STEO monthly path ending December 2027, so the result is directional, not volumetric. |

The June partial reopening supports only a modest revealed-ordering claim. Refinery runs
relaxed first (+1.5 mb/d), the U.S. stock-draw pace slowed (+0.77 mb/d across SPR and
commercial stocks), the global demand gap barely eased (+0.09 mb/d), and bypass did not
unwind. A 117 mb June increase in oil on water masked a 96 mb onshore draw; ordinary transit-
system refill explains 66-152 mb of that increase, base 111.6 mb. The apparent global stock
flip is therefore excluded from the ranking. This is suggestive, not causal: the reopening
was late in the month and cargo deliveries lagged.

## What cannot be responsibly parameterized

Historical shocks do not yield a transferable conservation half-life, universal rationing
threshold, bypass failure probability, Hormuz recovery time, foregone-build share or severity
index that can be inserted into this episode. The mechanisms and starting balances differ too
much. The balance sheet therefore reports computed zero and statutory bounds alongside a
separate unknown operational floor, and uses ordinal cost transitions where barrel allocation
is not defensible.
