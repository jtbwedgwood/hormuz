# Historical oil accounting for the Hormuz disruption, March-July 2026

**Research status:** July-vintage reconstruction preserved for audit. The evidence cutoff was
refreshed on **18 August 2026** to the **11 August EIA STEO** and **12 August IEA OMR**.
The August-vintage headline accounting and replacement values are in
`hormuz-what-happened-to-the-barrels.md`; figures below retain their July-vintage labels and
must not be quoted as current. Oil only. No price forecast.

**Original reconstruction cutoffs:** Gulf country production and route estimates are observed
or preliminary through **30 June**. The July-vintage EIA global balance and country-demand
comparison extends through **31 July**, where July was a forecast completed on 1 July. IEA
collective emergency releases were observed in aggregate through **21 July**; U.S. stocks were
observed through **24 July**. See the August synthesis linked above for updated cutoffs.

## Bottom line

The most defensible historical headline is:

> Between 1 March and 30 June 2026, global oil supply was roughly **1.40-1.44 billion barrels below pre-war expectations**. Extending the consistent EIA comparison through 31 July raises the estimate to **1.724 billion barrels**, but the July increment is still forecast and cannot yet be allocated credibly among Gulf producers.

This is not one clean causal estimate. The 1.724 billion barrels is the difference between EIA's frozen February supply forecast and its July-vintage March-July supply path. It includes the Hormuz shock, ordinary data revisions, and some taxonomy/model change. The route and country evidence nevertheless indicates that the Hormuz closure was overwhelmingly the dominant cause of the March-June loss.

A single pie containing rerouting, replacement production, strategic stocks, commercial stocks, and demand would double count. The correct reconstruction has two stages:

1. **Physical supply:** how missing Hormuz flows, bypass routes, Gulf shut-ins, and non-Gulf production produced the net global supply loss.
2. **Market clearing:** how lower consumption and inventories absorbed that already-measured supply loss.

In compact form, the matched March-June physical evidence says: **1,924.60 million barrels of expected Hormuz flow went missing; 362.10 million was offset by incremental Gulf bypasses and 56.90 million by net production gains outside the affected Gulf, leaving 1,505.60 million barrels of route-implied disruption.** That is 64.13 million barrels above the EIA global supply shortfall of 1,441.48 million, a manageable 4.4% route/taxonomy/timing residual. The market-clearing side then accounts for the same 1,441.48 million as **439.23 million of lower consumption, 396.08 million of expected inventory build that did not happen, and 606.17 million of implied inventory draw**. This is the closest defensible version of the requested all-the-`XX`-values-add-up narrative; rerouting belongs only in the first equation, while SPR and demand belong only in the second.

## Stage A: what happened to the Gulf barrels through June

At a roughly 20 mb/d pre-war Hormuz flow, about **2.42 billion barrels** would have crossed the Strait during March-June. The public flow evidence implies that approximately **1.925 billion barrels of that expected transit did not occur**.

Some oil still crossed the Strait: IEA estimates Hormuz oil flows averaged 2.7 mb/d in March-May, while the June route reconstruction implies approximately 8.9 mb/d after the temporary relaxation. Some oil also moved through pipelines and terminals outside the Strait.

### Rerouting and bypasses

The base reconstruction estimates **825.7 million barrels of gross non-Hormuz Gulf exports** during March-June. Most of those routes were already carrying oil before the closure, so only **362.1 million barrels** represent incremental rerouting versus the working pre-war route baseline. A reasonable sensitivity range is 300-430 million barrels.

| Producer and route | Gross March-June flow | Incremental flow above baseline | Evidence and interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Saudi Arabia: East-West/Petroline to Yanbu | 563.5m bbl | **319.5m bbl** | IEA says Yanbu exports rose from about 2 mb/d before the war to more than 5 mb/d in early June. The monthly path is calibrated rather than observed cargo by cargo. |
| UAE: Habshan-Fujairah pipeline | 210.3m | **76.1m** | The route can carry about 1.8 mb/d. UAE total exports rose from 1.9 mb/d in March to 4.3 mb/d in early June, but total exports also include stocks and limited Strait/STS activity. |
| Iraq-Türkiye pipeline to Ceyhan | 26.3m | **26.3m** | North Oil Company reported 250 kb/d starting 18 March. This was small relative to Iraq's lost southern exports. |
| Other routes and baseline calibration | 25.6m | **-59.8m** | This is not negative physical flow. It is the reconciliation needed because the named Saudi/UAE/Iraq estimates exceed the aggregate increase from the working pre-war bypass baseline. |
| **Total** | **825.7m** | **362.1m** | Incremental total is the amount that mitigated the closure; gross total should not be added to it. |

Subtracting the 362.1 million incremental bypass estimate from the 1.925 billion missing Hormuz flow leaves about **1.563 billion barrels of route-implied disruption** through June. The route calculation is a diagnostic rather than a clean production identity: exports can be delayed into storage, drawn from storage, consumed locally, or measured under different product definitions.

### Production actually shut in

EIA's July STEO provides the strongest country-resolved public estimate of crude production shut in through June:

| Gulf producer | February crude production | March-June crude shut-in | Share of country-resolved shut-ins |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Saudi Arabia | 10.50 mb/d | **359.80m bbl** | 30.4% |
| Iraq | 4.40 mb/d | **358.83m** | 30.3% |
| Kuwait | 2.56 mb/d | **215.83m** | 18.2% |
| UAE | 3.60 mb/d | **121.20m** | 10.2% |
| Qatar | 0.557 mb/d | **57.95m** | 4.9% |
| Iran | 3.39 mb/d | **52.51m** | 4.4% |
| Bahrain | 0.193 mb/d | **17.37m** | 1.5% |
| **Total** | **25.20 mb/d** | **1,183.49m bbl** | **100%** |

Monthly crude shut-ins were 8.89 mb/d in March, 10.40 in April, 11.20 in May, and 8.29 in June. The June decline is important: the historical series preserves the partial reopening rather than retroactively imposing a constant closure.

Crude is not all oil. A rough integration of IEA's broader affected-Gulf total-oil estimates gives about **1.518 billion barrels lost through June**, including condensates, NGLs, and other liquids. That number is a lower-confidence cross-check and must not be added to the EIA crude figure or the route-implied disruption.

Storage explains part of the route/production difference. Gulf producers added about **120 million barrels** to storage in March: 100 million floating and 20 million onshore. The 100 million was a **regional Gulf** floating build nested inside a 117 million barrel *decline* in global oil on water, not a global floating build. In June, exports rose 6.5 mb/d while production rose only 3.5 mb/d; a low-confidence reconciliation attributes roughly **60 million barrels** of June exports to oil released from Gulf storage and cargo timing.

### Supply added elsewhere

Compared with the frozen February forecast, the Americas produced **119.98 million barrels more** during March-July:

| Supplier | March-July production revision |
|---|---:|
| United States | **+77.98m bbl** |
| Brazil | **+35.48m** |
| Guyana | +3.60m |
| Mexico | +3.48m |
| Argentina | +3.13m |
| Canada | **-1.54m** |

Other regions partly offset those gains. Russia was 39.11 million barrels below the February path, Malaysia 9.31 million below, India 9.13 million below, and Norway 6.80 million below. The complete net production cushion outside the Middle East was therefore only **59.00 million barrels** through July, plus about 2.91 million from Oman if it is shown separately as an outside-Hormuz supplier.

For the matched March-June physical bridge, the comparable offsets are **54.38 million barrels** outside the Middle East plus **2.51 million** from Oman. The base route diagnostic then closes as:

> 1,924.60m gross missing Hormuz flow - 362.10m incremental bypass - 54.38m net non-Middle-East supply - 2.51m Oman supply = **1,441.48m global supply shortfall + 64.13m route/taxonomy/timing residual**.

Varying bypass between 300 and 430 million barrels moves that residual from 126 million to approximately zero. This is not a confidence interval; it shows the precision limit of combining transit, export, and production datasets.

The IEA's much larger **3.5 mb/d** increase in Atlantic Basin crude exports to East of Suez is not new production. It mixes incremental production, U.S. SPR and industry-stock releases, and diversion of existing cargoes from other buyers. Likewise, Canada's provisional 23.6 million barrel production contribution is an announcement, not demonstrated incremental supply: EIA's latest vintage instead puts Canadian supply slightly below forecast.

## Stage B: how the global market absorbed the shortage

The exact EIA forecast-vintage accounting through July is:

> **1,724.364m barrels lower supply = 566.356m lower consumption + 479.227m of expected stock build that did not occur + 678.781m implied inventory draw.**

| Top-level component | Million barrels | Share | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Consumption below the frozen February forecast | **566.36** | 32.8% | Includes shortage, lower refinery/product availability, conservation, price/macro effects, and ordinary forecast revisions. |
| Expected inventory build that did not occur | **479.23** | 27.8% | Counterfactual cushion: the February forecast expected substantial stock accumulation. These barrels were never physically released from tanks. |
| Implied inventory draw | **678.78** | 39.4% | Supply minus consumption in the EIA balance. This is not the same as observed tank movement. |
| **Total lower supply** | **1,724.36** | **100%** | March-June preliminary; July forecast. |

### Why the inventory number remains uncertain

A project composite of the latest explicit public IEA monthly stock headlines shows a net **298 million barrel draw in March-June**: 129 million in March, 117 million in April, 73 million in May, followed by a 21 million build in June. These values come from different report vintages, and IEA revised individual preliminary months by tens of millions of barrels; 298 million is therefore not a single published cumulative IEA series. EIA's supply-demand balance implies a **606.17 million barrel draw** over the same four months. The **308.17 million barrel diagnostic difference** combines stock coverage, vintage, timing, model, and statistical effects. It should not be described as hidden SPR or allocated mechanically to China.

Separating accessibility from ownership gives a different but non-additive view. Global oil
on water changed by -117 mb in March, +53 mb in April, an unknown May amount bounded here at
±35 mb, and +117 mb in June. The corresponding onshore-accessible draw is **316 / 351 / 386
mb**, compared with the 298 mb total-stock draw. These extra accessible barrels consumed are
important for durability, and voyage float is not usable headroom. They do **not** reduce the
308.17 mb same-bound residual: EIA's implied change and IEA's observed comparator both include
oil on water, so removing it from one side alone changes the inventory boundary.

June's +117 mb is also not evidence of deliberate storage by itself. A mechanical refill of
the ordinary Hormuz transit system after partial reopening accounts for **66 / 111.6 / 151.8
mb**, base 95% of the headline after capping at 117. Persistent longer rerouting separately
requires roughly **57-125 mb**, base 91 mb, of voyage float by end-June. Strong backwardation
weighs against profitable carry storage, but blocked Gulf cargo, congestion, sanctioned-fleet
dwell and measurement effects remain possible.

IEA reported about **290 million barrels of emergency oil actually released through 21 July**. For a 31 July chart, the project currently uses a 290/315/330 million barrel low/base/high range. Under the 315 million base case, the remaining **363.78 million barrels** of EIA's implied draw are non-emergency, unobserved, and balancing inventory adjustment. Calling all of this remaining amount a commercial stock draw would overstate what is observed.

Important dated implementation evidence includes:

- **United States:** 172.2 million barrels authorized on 11 March. DOE exchange awards were 45.2 million on 20 March, 8.5 million on 10 April, 26.0 million on 17 April, and 53.3 million on 11 May. DOE reported about 35 million delivered by 11 May. Exchange barrels have contractual return obligations.
- **Japan:** private stock-obligation relief began 16 March; the first national-site release began 26 March and a second schedule began 1 May. National, private, and producer-country joint stocks had fallen a combined 58.75 million barrels by 30 April.
- **Collective action:** IEA reported the cross-country delivered total of approximately 290 million barrels on 21 July. There is no official executed country table matching that aggregate.

### The U.S. counterfactual is especially clear

From 27 February through 24 July:

- U.S. SPR stocks fell **107.791 million barrels**. The frozen February STEO instead projected a **6.950 million barrel build** over March-July. The change-versus-change swing was therefore **114.741 million barrels**.
- Total U.S. commercial crude and petroleum-product stocks excluding the SPR fell **50.187 million barrels**, versus a projected **59.755 million barrel build**. That swing was **109.942 million barrels**.
- Actual government plus commercial stocks supplied **157.978 million barrels**. Including expected builds that never occurred, the total U.S. stock-path swing was **224.683 million barrels**, approximately 13% of the global March-July supply revision.

The U.S. SPR draw is already part of the IEA collective release. Commercial crude is already inside total commercial petroleum stocks. The 224.683 million barrel swing crosses two global buckets--actual draw and foregone build--and must not be added as a new top-level slice.

China is much less observable. IEA reports a 40 million barrel tank build in March and a 41 million draw in June, only a 1 million net draw across those two known months. April, May, and July ownership-resolved changes are unavailable. Public evidence supports commercial and operational stock flexibility, but not a separately measured large government-SPR release.

## Where consumption fell

The 566.356 million barrel March-July consumption gap decomposes exactly by EIA region:

| Region | Consumption below February forecast | Material country detail |
|---|---:|---|
| Asia and Oceania | **326.90m bbl** | China 119.99m; India 41.14m; Japan 19.52m; residual Asia/Oceania 146.25m |
| Middle East | **173.50m** | Country split unavailable; local refinery/feedstock outages and mobility/industrial disruption are likely major drivers |
| Africa | **35.10m** | Country split unavailable |
| Europe | **29.70m** | Regional total; not EU-only |
| Eurasia | **9.43m** | Russia 9.06m |
| Central and South America | **8.36m** | Brazil 9.58m, offset by -1.22m elsewhere |
| North America | **-16.63m** | U.S. -5.56m; Canada -10.14m; Mexico -0.94m--consumption was above the frozen path |
| **World** | **566.36m** | Exact regional closure |

South Korea is not a separate EIA country row. A deliberately low-confidence estimate assigns **7.65-61.20 million barrels**, with a 30.60 million base, as a suballocation of the 146.25 million residual Asian gap. It is not an additional contribution.

The demand revisions are not synonymous with voluntary conservation. Likely drivers include product shortages, refinery and petrochemical feedstock constraints, lower aviation, industrial disruption, higher costs, macroeconomic revisions, and policy restraint.

China provides the clearest mechanism evidence. Official crude-processing data imply year-over-year shortfalls of approximately **24.66 million barrels in April, 39.42 million in May, and 80.78 million in June**. These figures explain why crude imports and refinery intake fell, but they are not additional end-user demand reductions: refinery runs, product inventories, imports, and final consumption are different parts of the same national balance.

## Why there is no separate renewable-energy slice

China's June data show NEV production up 29.4% year over year and solar generation up 14.2%. But wind generation fell 5.6% and thermal generation still rose 0.5%. More importantly:

- China uses little oil to generate electricity, so extra wind or solar mainly displaces coal or gas rather than petroleum.
- Installed capacity, generation, vehicle production, vehicle sales, and actual oil displacement are different measures.
- Rapid EV and renewable adoption was already embedded in the frozen February oil-demand forecast. The accounting needs the incremental adoption caused by the post-February shock, not the entire structural trend.
- The IEA policy tracker documents actions in nearly 80 countries but does not publish a de-duplicated realized oil-savings figure.

The defensible conclusion is therefore: renewables, EVs, electrification, and fuel switching probably made demand more flexible, but the available data cannot isolate an additional March-July barrel contribution. Any realized saving remains inside the measured country demand gap or the residual.

## Remaining unknowns and plausible explanations

The largest unresolved quantities are not empty knowledge; they have bounded interpretations:

1. **July Gulf production by country.** The 282.89 million barrel July contribution to the EIA global shortfall is forecast. The latest country-resolved Gulf production data end in June, and the July forecast was completed before the 7-8 July escalation.
2. **The route/taxonomy residual.** The matched March-June base residual is 64.13 million barrels, with a roughly zero-to-126 million sensitivity to bypass assumptions. It covers crude-versus-total-oil definitions, Gulf storage, domestic use, cargo timing, and incomplete route data.
3. **The stock/model discrepancy.** A mixed-vintage composite of IEA observed-stock headlines shows 308.17 million fewer barrels drawn through June than EIA's balance implies. Likely contributors include preliminary-data revisions, incomplete non-OECD tank coverage, bonded and commercial Chinese stocks, and supply/demand estimation errors. Reclassifying observed oil on water as inaccessible closes zero barrels on the matched total-inventory boundary, although separate measurement and timing error can still contribute.
4. **Country emergency execution.** The 290 million barrel aggregate is credible; most of the country split outside the U.S., Japan, and Italy is inferred from plans because national execution series are incomplete.
5. **Causal demand attribution.** The regional totals close, but public data cannot allocate every barrel among forced shortage, price response, conservation, macro weakness, refinery constraints, or electrification.

These unknowns should remain visible rather than being back-allocated to a preferred story.

A deliberately lower-confidence follow-on investigation now develops bounded stories for the inventory and demand residuals in `docs/hormuz-inventory-and-demand-residual-stories.md`. Its low/base/high allocations are scenario accounting, not revisions to the historical headline.

`docs/hormuz-what-happened-to-the-barrels.md` re-partitions this same bridge by evidence quality and reports the size of the unknown bucket as a number: on the matched March-June frame, 10.9% of the net supply loss is directly observed, 27.5% is reasonably assumed, 34.2% is educated guess, and 27.5% is unknown. It also splits the implied inventory draw into the 298 million observed and the 308.171 million balance plug at the top level, so that model error is not booked twice, and presents both stages as one waist-node Sankey.
