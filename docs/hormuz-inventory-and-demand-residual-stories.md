# Plausible stories for the remaining inventory and demand residuals

**Research status:** hypothesis-building draft as of 4 August 2026. Oil only. This document deliberately separates observation from scenario allocation.

## Questions

1. What could explain the 308.171 million barrel March-June gap between EIA's implied inventory draw and IEA's observed global stock change?
2. What mechanisms plausibly explain the regional oil-consumption revisions: rapid substitution, explicit conservation policy, autonomous behavioral response, forced shortage or activity loss, structural trends, or ordinary forecast error?

## Evidence labels

- **Observed:** a measured stock, flow, consumption, run-rate, or dated policy action.
- **Attributed view:** a named analyst, agency, firm, or news source's interpretation.
- **Project inference:** our explanation linking observed facts; it can be plausible without being uniquely identified.
- **Scenario allocation:** a low/base/high numerical story that closes arithmetically but is not a new measurement.

## Mystery 1: the stock gap is a coverage-and-model gap, not automatically a hidden draw

For March-June, the EIA supply-demand balance implies a 606.171 million barrel inventory draw while a project composite of public IEA monthly observed-stock headlines sums to 298 million barrels. The 308.171 million barrel difference combines at least three conceptually different possibilities:

1. physical stock changes outside the IEA's observable coverage;
2. timing and location differences, especially oil moving between tanks and ships; and
3. error or later revision in the independently estimated supply and consumption series that create EIA's implied balance.

The IEA's own glossary says non-OECD stocks and some secondary and tertiary OECD industry stocks are not specifically accounted for in its main balance and therefore enter its miscellaneous-to-balance category. This makes non-reported commercial stocks a genuine candidate, but also means the residual cannot be read as a tank-level observation.

The **298 million barrel comparator is itself less solid than it initially appeared**. It combines the latest explicit public monthly values available from different OMR vintages. Preliminary IEA estimates changed materially: March moved from an 85 million barrel draw to 129 million, April from 117 million to 74 million, and May from 143 million to 73 million. The public summaries do not expose a fully revised July-vintage March-June series. Thus 308.171 million is a useful diagnostic, but not a physical discrepancy known to the nearest million barrels.

### The U.S. commercial draw is separate from emergency releases but not necessarily absent from observed stocks

The approximately 290 million barrel IEA figure is oil released under the coordinated emergency action. U.S. commercial inventories are not part of that program total unless they were obligated industry stocks specifically released through the action. The project's measured **50.187 million barrel** decline in total U.S. commercial petroleum stocks through 24 July is therefore a distinct mechanism from the U.S. SPR draw.

For the matched March-June residual, the relevant U.S. commercial draw is **67.317 million barrels**. The U.S. SPR drew another **89.786 million**, for a combined **157.103 million barrels**, equivalent to about 52.7% of the 298 million project composite before IEA standardization and endpoint differences.

It does not follow that those commercial barrels explain part of the 308.171 million coverage gap. IEA observed global stocks include OECD industry stocks and its preliminary U.S. estimates explicitly use EIA weekly and monthly data. The U.S. commercial decline is therefore largely inside the IEA observed total. The observed total is a **net** change: commercial and government draws in one place can be offset by tank or floating-stock builds elsewhere.

June illustrates the problem. IEA reported about 96 million barrels of continued onshore draw, including 44 million from OECD government stocks, but oil on water increased by 117 million barrels. Global observed stocks therefore registered a 21 million barrel build even while consumers were relying on oil released from tanks.

### Competing China stories to test

The public evidence supports at least three live hypotheses:

- **Commercial-draw story:** refiners used visible commercial or operational inventories while cutting imports and refinery runs. Vortexa, Kpler, Energy Aspects, and Kayrros estimates reported in June pointed to roughly 1 mb/d of prospective commercial draw and about 25 million barrels drawn in the month to 7 June.
- **Hidden-SPR-transfer story:** less-visible underground government stocks may have replenished visible commercial facilities. Kpler analyst Sumit Ritolia said this could not be fully ruled out. This mechanism would make a commercial draw visible without revealing the original government source.
- **No-major-SPR-draw story:** Kpler's observed onshore data indicated that China's SPR continued building or was largely untouched during important parts of the shock, while refinery intake and demand fell instead. IEA's known monthly observations show a 40 million barrel Chinese build in March and a 41 million draw in June, close to net zero across those two months.

These stories are not fully mutually exclusive. China could simultaneously draw refinery working stocks, transfer some government barrels between facilities, keep other strategic sites building, reduce runs, and accumulate imported cargoes on water.

### Exact-closing inventory scenarios

The following allocations partition the 308.171 million barrel diagnostic. They are scenarios, not estimates. “Low” and “high” refer to the amount assigned to hidden physical stocks.

| Candidate mechanism | Low hidden physical | Base mixed | High hidden physical |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| China underground government/NOC net draw not visible elsewhere | 0.000m bbl | **20.000m** | 75.000m |
| Other non-OECD unreported primary-stock draw | 30.000m | **70.000m** | 110.000m |
| Excluded secondary, tertiary and end-user stocks | 10.000m | **25.000m** | 35.000m |
| Oil-on-water and cargo timing/measurement differences | 20.000m | **35.000m** | 40.000m |
| IEA preliminary-vintage, standardization and cutoff mismatch | 35.000m | **35.000m** | 35.000m |
| EIA supply, demand, taxonomy and balance error | 213.171m | **123.171m** | 13.171m |
| **Total diagnostic residual** | **308.171m** | **308.171m** | **308.171m** |
| **Memo: candidate hidden physical draw** | **40.000m** | **115.000m** | **220.000m** |

The base story—115 million physical and 193.171 million timing/model—is preferred because it gives weight to opaque non-OECD inventories without ignoring the demonstrated IEA revisions and analyst warnings that refinery runs and demand may be overstated. The 220 million hidden-physical case is possible, but it requires visible systems to have missed a very large draw while Chinese tanks were initially building. The 40 million case may put too much faith in the EIA balance.

### Other mechanisms to test

- non-OECD commercial and refinery stocks in India, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Latin America;
- obligated industry stocks outside the public-government category;
- oil on water drawn early in the shock, followed by a large June rebuild as delayed cargoes and rerouted Atlantic barrels spent longer in transit;
- bonded, pipeline, refinery, petrochemical-feedstock, and end-user stocks not captured consistently across datasets;
- changes in statistical discrepancy caused by unusually rapid shifts in supply, refinery runs, exports, and consumption;
- differences between crude-only tracking and the all-liquids/product scope of the global balance.

## Mystery 2: lower oil consumption is not synonymous with clean energy substitution

Rapid fuel switching is physically possible only in a subset of oil uses. Oil is dominant in road transport, aviation, shipping, petrochemical feedstocks, and some industrial processes; these generally cannot switch to wind, solar, coal, gas, or electricity within a few months without existing dual-fuel equipment, spare public transit, behavioral change, or lower output. Conversely, oil-fired power generation and some industrial boilers can switch relatively quickly where alternative capacity and fuel are already connected.

The accounting therefore uses five mechanisms:

1. **Fast substitution:** an existing vehicle, boiler, generator, plant, or transport mode uses a non-oil source instead.
2. **Conservation and efficiency:** the same service is delivered with less oil through fewer trips, slower speeds, telework, carpooling, public transit, or operating changes.
3. **Forced shortage and activity loss:** the service or output does not occur, including grounded flights, refinery/petrochemical curtailment, rationing, and lost industrial activity.
4. **Structural transition already underway:** EVs, electrification, efficiency, and renewables continue displacing oil, but only acceleration beyond the frozen February forecast belongs to the shock response.
5. **Forecast/model revision:** weather, macro conditions, seasonal adjustment, product reclassification, and revisions unrelated to Hormuz.

The low/base/high stories in this document are scenario allocations of measured regional forecast revisions across those mechanisms. They are not additional demand estimates and must sum back to the existing regional totals.

## Asia and Oceania: a 326.900 million barrel revision

The strongest physical conclusion is negative: Asia did not replace hundreds of millions of barrels with newly generated renewable electricity in five months. Petroleum supplied only about 0.1% of Chinese electricity, less than 1% in India, and about 3% in Japan before the shock. New wind and solar therefore mostly displace coal or gas. Oil savings arise indirectly through already-installed EVs, electric rail, electric cooking, LNG/electric trucks, public transit and behavioral changes.

The preferred regional scenario is:

| Mechanism | Low-switching | Base | High-switching |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Incremental switching/electrification beyond February path | 15.730m bbl | **39.633m** | 74.334m |
| Voluntary conservation and efficiency | 33.083m | **52.563m** | 63.973m |
| Forced refinery, feedstock and product constraints | 127.431m | **100.652m** | 76.135m |
| Activity and macroeconomic loss | 65.493m | **59.849m** | 49.717m |
| Structural trend and ordinary forecast revision | 68.819m | **57.036m** | 46.395m |
| Unresolved | 16.345m | **17.168m** | 16.345m |
| **Asia/Oceania total** | **326.900m** | **326.900m** | **326.900m** |

The base assigns only 12.1% to switching/electrification. The largest single mechanism is forced refinery, naphtha/petrochemical and product constraint. An alternative, non-additive attribution lens assigns **32.494 million barrels to explicit direct restraint**, **89.688 million to decentralized response**, **113.567 million to forced supply or industrial constraint**, and **91.151 million to structural revision or unknown**.

### Country stories

- **China, 119.992m:** base allocation 24.0 switching/electrification, 24.0 conservation, 36.0 forced refinery/feedstock constraint, 18.0 activity loss, 12.0 structural/revision and 6.0 unresolved. China already had a large EV, high-speed-rail and electric/LNG-truck system, making marginal transport switching plausible. But price caps, slow fleet turnover, export restrictions and extraordinary refinery-run cuts argue against crediting most of the gap to clean substitution.
- **India, 41.141m:** base allocation 3.29 switching, 4.11 conservation, 7.41 forced shortage/feedstock, 10.29 activity loss, 13.17 ordinary revision and 2.88 unresolved. May-June gasoline and diesel were much more resilient than LPG, naphtha and bitumen, pointing toward cooking-fuel rationing, petrochemical/construction losses and weaker growth rather than mass road-fuel substitution. Modi's public-transport, carpooling, telework and travel appeals are real explicit policy, but fuel subsidies and tax cuts muted the price signal.
- **Japan, 19.522m:** base allocation 1.56 switching, 2.34 conservation, 8.78 forced refinery/naphtha constraint, 2.93 activity, 2.93 trend/revision and 0.98 unresolved. April naphtha sales fell much more than gasoline, and fuel subsidies limited voluntary price response; the dominant story is petrochemical/feedstock constraint.
- **South Korea, provisional 30.60m:** base allocation 1.53 switching, 2.45 conservation, 13.77 forced refinery/feedstock constraint, 5.51 activity, 5.81 trend/revision and 1.53 unresolved. Korea is a bounded suballocation of the remaining Asian total, not an EIA country observation. Its unusually large naphtha/petrochemical sector makes feedstock curtailment more credible than household conservation.
- **Other Asia/Oceania, 115.646m after the Korea base:** Southeast Asia had the clearest explicit restraint policies—telework, reduced travel, public-transport promotion, shortened work or school weeks, purchase controls and rationing—but also subsidies and price caps. The base assigns 9.25 switching, 19.66 conservation, 34.69 forced constraint, 23.13 activity loss, 23.13 forecast/revision and 5.78 unresolved.

The country values are nested in the regional total. Refinery-run cuts, import losses, cracker outages, product sales and policy announcements are mechanism evidence, not extra barrels to add.

## Middle East, Africa, Europe and other regions

### Middle East: 173.496 million barrels

This is best understood as a scarcity and activity-loss story, not a clean substitution story. The base allocation is **65.0 million barrels of forced refinery/feedstock/LPG scarcity, 38.0 million of aviation and security-related mobility loss, 30.0 million of trade/tourism/logistics and macro loss, 12.0 million of autonomous price response, 10.0 million of explicit conservation policy, 4.0 million of rapid oil switching, and 14.496 million residual**.

The regional magnitude is physically plausible. Pre-shock Middle East oil demand was about 8.85 mb/d, led by Saudi Arabia, Iran, UAE, Iraq, Kuwait and Qatar. Gulf production and export-refinery outages disrupted domestic feedstocks and product availability at the same time that flight cancellations, security restrictions, trade and tourism weakened activity. The rapid-switching allocation is only 2.3% of the regional gap.

### Africa: 35.100 million barrels

Africa's oil use is transport-fuel-heavy and many countries depend on imported refined products and scarce foreign exchange. The base allocation is **20.0 million barrels of forced transport-fuel/import scarcity, 5.0 million of trade/logistics and macro loss, 3.5 million of LPG/kerosene affordability and fuel stacking, 2.5 million of explicit conservation, 2.0 million of price response, 1.0 million of switching, and 1.100 million residual**.

Kpler's East and Southern Africa analysis emphasizes diesel; Egypt provides dated evidence of shop-hour, lighting and remote-work measures. But South Africa initially reported no immediate physical shortage and used fiscal relief, so a continent-wide rationing narrative would be too strong.

### Europe: 29.701 million barrels

Europe's gap is smaller and more aviation-heavy. The base assigns **10.0 million barrels to aviation/jet constraints, 4.0 million to explicit policy conservation, 4.0 million to price response, 3.0 million to macro/industrial/trade loss, 2.0 million to road-freight/product scarcity, 1.5 million to switching, and 5.201 million residual**.

The European Commission issued fuel-saving guidance and transport-sector flexibility measures, but was still reporting no general EU fuel shortage in May and identified jet fuel as the main concern. Its larger annual technical-savings estimates are not evidence that those savings occurred by July.

### Smaller regions and the North American counterexample

- **Eurasia, 9.433m lower:** 4.5 refinery/product constraint, 1.3 macro/logistics, 0.8 price, 0.5 aviation, 0.5 policy, 0.2 switching and 1.633 residual. Russia accounts for nearly all of the regional EIA gap, and Russia-Ukraine-war refinery disruption overlaps Hormuz attribution.
- **Central and South America, 8.359m lower:** 1.5 price response, 1.2 macro/logistics, 0.8 switching, 0.7 policy, 0.7 aviation, 0.5 scarcity and 2.959 residual. Brazil's country gap exceeds the regional total because consumption elsewhere revised upward.
- **North America, 16.633m higher than forecast:** 6.0 fiscal price shielding/tax relief, 3.0 U.S. product mix and seasonality, 3.0 Canada/Mexico baseline revision, 2.0 energy/export activity and 2.633 residual. Canada suspended federal gasoline, diesel and aviation fuel excise taxes. U.S. gasoline and residual-fuel revisions were positive while distillate and jet were negative, so even this counterexample is not simply “drivers ignored prices.”

## One global scenario that closes

To answer the policy-versus-behavior question cleanly, the regional base cases can be cross-walked into four broad, mutually exclusive explanations. Before the North American offset, regions with lower consumption have a gross **582.989 million barrel** gap:

| Explanatory class | Gross downward contribution | Share of gross gap | What it means |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Explicit government demand restraint | **50.194m bbl** | 8.6% | Telework, travel reduction, conservation campaigns, purchase controls and rationing—not stock releases, subsidies or export restrictions that preserve demand. |
| Decentralized response and rapid switching | **117.488m** | 20.2% | Household/firm price response plus use of already-installed electric, biofuel, transit or dual-fuel alternatives. |
| Forced supply, refinery/feedstock and activity constraint | **298.767m** | 51.2% | Unavailable fuel/feedstocks, grounded aviation, refinery/petrochemical curtailment, lost freight, tourism, trade and output. |
| Structural trend, ordinary forecast revision and unknown | **116.540m** | 20.0% | Pre-existing transition, model revision and unallocated uncertainty. |
| **Gross regions with lower consumption** | **582.989m** | **100%** | Exact scenario closure. |
| North American upward revision | **-16.633m** | — | Price shielding, activity, product mix and baseline revision offset the global decline. |
| **World net demand gap** | **566.356m** | — | Matches the EIA March-July accounting identity. |

The most important implication is that **only about 8.6% of the gross downward revision is plausibly attributed directly to explicit restraint policy in the base case**. Roughly one-fifth is decentralized response or rapid switching, but more than half is involuntary scarcity or lost economic activity. This is a disciplined story rather than causal identification; reasonable low/high cases move barrels among the four classes.

The direct oil-switching component is smaller still. Adding the base rapid-switching allocations across regions with lower consumption gives about **47.1 million barrels**, roughly 8.1% of their gross gap. Even this includes behavioral use of pre-existing electrification and other alternatives, not solely new renewable generation.

## Follow-on

`docs/hormuz-what-happened-to-the-barrels.md` uses these scenarios but changes one framing choice deliberately. The 308.171 million barrel residual is treated there as a top-level absorption slice labelled unknown, rather than being allocated across mechanisms, because the base scenario's ~123 million barrel "EIA supply, demand, taxonomy and balance error" line double counts model error that EIA's implied draw already contains. The demand mechanism shares in this document are carried over to the March-June frame proportionally and are the basis for classing roughly 88 million barrels of the demand gap as structural trend or ordinary revision rather than shock response.
